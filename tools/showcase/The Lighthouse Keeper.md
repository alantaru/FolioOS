# The Lighthouse Keeper

## I. The Letter

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which was strange, because nothing ever arrived on Tuesdays. The boat came on Fridays — when the sea allowed it — and the gulls brought nothing but noise.

Elena turned the envelope over twice before opening it. The handwriting was her mother's, slanted and impatient, the letters leaning forward as if they were in a hurry to leave the page.

> *Come home before the winter. The house is too quiet, and so am I.*

She read it standing by the window, while the lamp above her turned its slow, patient circle over the water. Forty-one years of light, her grandfather used to say. Forty-one years and not one ship lost.

## II. The Storm

The storm came from the south, as the old ones always did. By noon the horizon had disappeared — first the line between sea and sky, then the sky itself, then the sea.

Elena climbed the hundred and twelve steps with the oil can in one hand and the logbook in the other. She counted them, as she did every night, because counting was a way of keeping the fear in order.

"One more night," she said to the empty room. "Just one more."

The wind answered by throwing rain against the glass like handfuls of gravel. Somewhere below, the door she had forgotten to latch began to knock — once, twice — and then fell silent, which was worse.

## III. The Ship

At three in the morning she saw it: a single light, low on the water, rising and falling where no light should be. A fishing boat, perhaps, or something larger that had lost its way.

She did the only thing a keeper can do. She cleaned the lens. She trimmed the wick. She stood beside the lamp and willed it brighter, as if light could be pushed by hand across the dark.

The small light hesitated. Then, slowly, it turned toward the harbor.

## IV. Home

In the spring she took the Friday boat to the mainland, with the letter folded in her coat and the logbook under her arm. The last entry was written in her careful, upright hand:

> *Night of the storm. One vessel, safe. Light never failed.*

Her mother was waiting at the end of the pier, smaller than she remembered, and louder, and exactly the same.
